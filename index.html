Understanding Multi-Temporal SAR Data (2017–2026)

Because the joint NASA-ISRO NISAR satellite launched in late 2025, continuous 7+ year historical SAR tracking (2017–2024) utilizes ESA's Sentinel-1 C-Band SAR (5.5 cm wavelength). Starting late 2025 and into 2026, high-resolution L-Band (24 cm) canopy-penetrating radar from NISAR provides sub-canopy structural baseline data of Mount Popa's volcanic cone.

NISAR & Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) Context

Why combine Sentinel-1 (2017–2024) and NISAR (2025–2026)?

The joint NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) satellite mission launched in July 2025. To analyze a continuous 7+ year timeline (e.g., from 2017 to present), Earth Observation scientists pair legacy C-Band SAR archives (such as Copernicus Sentinel-1) with new high-resolution NISAR L-band and S-band observations.

Key Concepts in Radar Remote Sensing:

  • All-Weather Active Microwave: SAR emits its own radio signals rather than relying on sunlight. It sees directly through heavy cloud cover and monsoons over Myanmar.
  • L-Band vs. C-Band Penetration: C-band (Sentinel-1, 5.5cm) reflects primarily off leaf canopies. NISAR's L-band (24cm) penetrates all the way through dense foliage to bounce off trunk structures and ground surfaces.
  • InSAR Interferometry: By measuring microwave phase differences between two satellite passes, InSAR can track ground displacement (landslides, magma chamber inflation, or fault shifts) with millimeter accuracy.
  • SweepSAR Technology: NISAR's 12-meter deployable mesh reflector allows continuous high-resolution imaging over a massive 240 km wide swath.
